    NCLEX PRIORITIES, SAFETY, AND CLINICAL JUDGMENT

 1. Clinical Judgment Is the Core of NCLEX-RN
    The Next Generation NCLEX focuses on how nurses
    think, not just what they know. Every question is rooted
    in the NCSBN Clinical Judgment Measurement Model
    (CJMM). You must consistently demonstrate the ability
    to:

        Recognize relevant cues
        Analyze patient data
        Prioritize problems
        Select safe, evidence-based actions
        Evaluate outcomes

    NCLEX questions often provide extra information. Your
    job is to identify what matters most and ignore
    distractions.

 2. Safety Always Comes First
    Patient safety is the highest priority on the NCLEX. If an
    answer choice prevents harm, it usually ranks higher
    than comfort, education, or documentation.

    Key safety principles tested repeatedly:

        Verify patient identity before medications or
        procedures
        Follow infection control standards (hand hygiene, PPE,
        isolation)
        Prevent falls and aspiration
        Monitor for medication side effects and adverse
        reactions
        Act immediately on critical lab values
When in doubt, choose the option that keeps the patient alive and safe.

                                   4
3. ABCs and Physiological Priorities

 NCLEX consistently uses ABCs (Airway, Breathing,
 Circulation) to test prioritization.
       Airway problems always come first
       Breathing problems are next
       Circulation issues follow
 Examples:
       A patient with chest pain and low oxygen saturation
       takes priority over pain or anxiety
       Treat airway obstruction before an abnormal lab
       values
       Hypoxia is treated before hypotension unless
       hemorrhage is present
 Maslow’s hierarchy reinforces this: physiological needs
 come before psychosocial needs.




4. Assessment Before Intervention (Unless Emergent)

 Most NCLEX questions require assessment first.
 Exceptions include:
       Cardiac arrest
       Active seizures
       Severe hypoglycemia
       Anaphylaxis
       Severe respiratory distress
 If the patient is unstable, act.
 If the patient is stable, assess first.




                                 5
5. Recognizing Deterioration
 You must recognize early signs of decline:

        Increasing respiratory rate
        Decreasing oxygen saturation
        New confusion or agitation
        Decreased urine output
        Worsening vital sign trends

 NCLEX rewards early intervention and escalation of
 care.

HIGH-RISK CONDITIONS, LABS, AND MEDICATION SAFETY

6. High-Risk Conditions You Must Know

  Certain conditions appear repeatedly because they are life-
  threatening:

  Respiratory

         Airway obstruction
         Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)
         Pneumothorax
         Pulmonary embolism

  Cardiovascular

         Acute coronary syndrome
         Heart failure exacerbation
         Cardiac tamponade
         Shock states

   Neurological

         Stroke (ischemic vs hemorrhagic)
         Increased intracranial pressure
         Seizures

   Endocrine

         Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) vs HHS
         Hypoglycemia vs Hyperglycemia
         Thyroid storm vs Hypothyroidism
         Cushing Syndrome vs Addison disease

    Infection

         Sepsis and septic shock

  You must know early signs, priority interventions, and what to
  monitor.




                              7
7. Must-Know Lab Values and Trends
   NCLEX tests labs in context, not memorization alone.

   Critical labs that require action:

         Potassium abnormalities
         Blood glucose extremes
         Elevated creatinine with low urine output
         ABG changes indicating acidosis or alkalosis
         Elevated lactate in sepsis

   Trend interpretation is key:

         A slowly rising creatinine suggests worsening
         kidney function
         Falling hemoglobin suggests bleeding
         Improving labs indicate effective treatment

   Never look at a single lab in isolation.

8. Medication Safety Is Heavily Tested

   Medication questions focus on safety, monitoring, and
   teaching, not memorizing drug lists.

   High-alert medications to know:

         Insulin
         Opioids
         Anticoagulants
         IV potassium
         Cardiac medications

   Key medication principles:

         Know hold parameters
         Monitor for adverse reactions
         Assess before giving medications
         Educate patients on side effects
         Use the rights of medication administration

   If a medication affects heart rate, blood pressure, or
   respiratory status, monitoring is critical. Record V/S before
   and after.
                                  8
9. Fluids and Electrolytes Matter
    You must understand:
         Isotonic vs hypotonic vs hypertonic fluids
         Signs of fluid overload vs dehydration
         Acid-base balance basics
    Examples:
         NS and LR are used for volume replacement
         Hypotonic fluids can worsen cerebral edema
         Hyperkalemia increases the risk of dysrhythmias
         D5W is used to treat hypernatremia because it
         provides free water to dilute excess sodium.
         D51/2NS is used for hydration, provides calories,
         and prevents catabolism.

    NCLEX tests why a fluid is chosen, not just the name.

DELEGATION, COMMUNICATION, AND NCLEX STRATEGY

10. Delegation and Scope of Practice
      Delegation questions are about patient stability and task
      complexity.

      UAP can:
              Take vital signs on stable patients
              Assist with hygiene
              Report findings
      LPN can:
              Administer oral medications
              Perform wound care
              Monitor stable patients
      RN must:
              Assess
              Teach
              Evaluate outcomes
              Care for unstable patients
   Never delegate assessment, teaching, or clinical judgment.

11. Communication and SBAR
      Effective communication is essential for patient safety.
      SBAR is frequently tested:
              Situation
              Background
              Assessment
              Recommendation
      When notifying a provider, include:
              Current vital signs
              Relevant labs
              Your nursing assessment
              A clear recommendation
   Incomplete communication is a common NCLEX trap.

                               10
12. Ethical and Legal Responsibilities
      NCLEX expects nurses to practice within ethical and
      legal standards.
      Key concepts:
             Informed consent
             Patient autonomy
             Confidentiality
             Mandatory reporting
             Documentation accuracy
If a patient refuses care, assess understanding and respect
their decision unless life-threatening.




13. Education and Patient Teaching
      Teaching questions focus on:
             Safety
             Medication adherence
             Disease management
             Discharge readiness
      Effective teaching:
             Uses simple language
             Includes return demonstration
             Addresses patient readiness to learn
Teaching is ineffective if the patient is unstable.

14. NCLEX Answer-Selection Strategy
      When stuck:

            Eliminate unsafe options
            Choose airway over everything else
            Pick assessment before intervention (unless
            emergent)
            Prioritize unstable patients
            Choose evidence-based care

      Avoid answers that:

            Delay care unnecessarily
            Are outside nursing scope
            Ignore safety
            Use absolute language like “always” or “never”

     NCLEX-RN success depends on:
            Strong clinical judgment
            Patient safety awareness
            Prioritization skills
            Understanding patterns, not memorization

 If you think like a safe, evidence-based nurse, you will pass.

Understanding Pulmonary Physiology and Examination

Ventilation is the movement of air in and out of the lungs to maintain
proper oxygen and carbon dioxide levels.

The brainstem regulates ventilation by detecting a decrease in blood pH
(acidosis)/low PaO2, which triggers an increase in the rate and depth of
breathing.

Peripheral regulation occurs in the aortic arch, where low blood oxygen
levels (hypoxemia/decrease PaO2) stimulate increased respiratory rate and
depth.

In patients with chronic hypercapnia (Chronic PaCO2), the drive to
breathe is triggered by low oxygen levels rather than high carbon
dioxide; carbon dioxide levels/PaCO2 reflect the effectiveness of
ventilation.

The diaphragm is the primary muscle responsible for respiration.

The optimal position for adequate ventilation is the upright
position.

A1. Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)

Despite low to normal pulmonary
capillary pressures, ARDS is a
noncardiogenic pulmonary edema. ARDS
patients are considered to have high-
permeability pulmonary edema rather
than cardiogenic pulmonary edema.
These patients have bilateral pulmonary
infiltrates and severe hypoxemia, but the
changes are not secondary to heart
failure. In cases of ARDS, the alveolar-
capillary membrane is impaired, leading
to interstitial and alveolar fluid and
protein leakage (Juarez, 2022).

A3. Pneumonia
 Pneumonia is an inflammatory condition of the lung interstitial where alveoli fill
 with fluid and blood cells. The disease process starts with the infection of the
 alveolar spaces. As the micromutant proliferates, the alveolar spaces become
 inundated with a mixture of fluid, white blood cells, and cellular debris from
 phagocytosis of the infectious agent. The infection spreads from the alveolus and
 may affect the distal airways (bronchopneumonia) or involve part of a lobe
 (lobular pneumonia) or an entire lung (lobar pneumonia) (Yamagata, Nakanishi,
 & Ishida, 2020).


                                           The inflammatory process leads to
                                           thickening of the alveolar wall, resulting
                                           in decreased lung compliance and,
                                           therefore, increased work of breathing.
                                           Among them is the flooding of fluid-
                                           filled alveoli, creating a physiological
                                           shunt venous blood passes through
                                           unventilated portions of lung tissue to
                                           be returned to the left atrium,
                                           unoxygenated. Pneumonia can cause
                                           complications, like respiratory failure,
                                           lung abscess, septic shock, and
                                           hypoxemia as well. Infection may
                                           transmit through the bloodstream,
                                           resulting in endocarditis, pericarditis,
                                           meningitis, or bacteremia (Yamagata,
                                           Nakanishi, & Ishida, 2020).

A4. Aspiration
 Aspiration occurs when harmful substances
 —food, liquid, vomit, or oral secretions are
 inhaled into the lungs, causing lung injury.
 Damage may result from the aspirated
 material's chemical, mechanical, or bacterial
 properties. Oropharyngeal aspiration is the
 most frequent type, which may be micro
 aspiration (minimal, often undetectable) or
 massive. It can cause inflammation that can
 lead to aspiration pneumonia or lung
 infections (Juarez, 2022).
                                                 Remember:

                                    **Most aspiration occurs in the right lung
                                   due to the right mainstem bronchus being
                                   shorter, wider, and with less of an angle.

Asthma
   Asthma is a chronic, often reversible form of
   obstructive lung disease characterized by
   inflammation of the airways and hyperreactivity to
   various triggers. That hyperresponsiveness results
   in airway obstruction from smooth muscle spasm,
   mucosal edema, and thick mucus secretion. These
   triggers may be allergens, infections (especially
   viral), and inhaled irritants. In allergic asthma, an
   immune response is triggered by immunoglobulin
   E (IgE), which releases mediators such as
   histamine and slow-reacting substances of
   anaphylaxis that induce bronchoconstriction and
   inflammation (Wang et al., 2020).

A10. Pneumothorax
Pneumothorax occurs when air collects in the pleural space, disrupting the normal
negative pressure that keeps the lungs expanded and resulting in partial or complete
collapse of the affected lung (atelectasis) and impaired gas exchange. Under normal
circumstances, the pleural space is a vacuum between the visceral and parietal
pleura. When air moves into this space, pressure builds, and the lung pulls back.
There are three primary forms of pneumothorax: spontaneous (non-traumatic, often
from ruptured air sacs), traumatic (due to injury, either closed or open, such as a rib
fracture or stab injury), and tension pneumothorax, a medical emergency in which
trapped air applies potentially lethal pressure to structures of the thorax (Hallifax et
al., 2020).

A11. Tuberculosis (TB)
 Tuberculosis is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, an aerobic, acid-fast
 bacillus. It mainly attacks the lungs, though it can also spread to other organs,
 including the brain, kidneys, bones, and lymph nodes (extrapulmonary TB). TB is
 spread via airborne droplets and continues to be one of the top infectious disease
 killers worldwide. In the lungs, TB can present as a type of pneumonia with
 cavities or progress to serious conditions such as widespread inflammation, tissue
 death, fluid buildup, and scarring. The disease involves a complicated connection
 between bacterial virulence factors and the host immune response (Leung et al.,
 2020).

 Cardiac tamponade is a medical emergency caused by the rapid

  Infectious and inflammatory conditions

C1. GERD
Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a chronic condition caused by the
inappropriate relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES),
allowing the backward flow of gastric acid and pepsin
into the esophagus. While occasional reflux is normal,
persistent episodes damage the esophageal mucosa,
leading to inflammation, erosion, and complications
such as esophagitis, bleeding, ulceration, and strictures
that cause difficulty swallowing. In some cases, the
normal squamous epithelium is replaced by intestinal
columnar epithelium, a condition known as Barrett
esophagus, which is a premalignant change seen in 10–
15% of GERD patients and may progress to adenocarcinoma. GERD may also
result in respiratory issues (e.g., cough, asthma, bronchitis), dental erosion, and
sleep apnea. Although the exact cause is unclear, contributing factors include
delayed gastric emptying, obesity, aging, and substances (dietary or
pharmacologic) that reduce lower esophageal sphincter(LES) tone. Despite many
patients having normal acid levels and LES pressure, the recurrent acid exposure
exceeds the esophagus’s protective capacity, leading to significant morbidity if
untreated (Yadlapati & Pandolfino, 2020).

C2. Pancreatitis
 Pancreatitis is an inflammatory condition of the pancreas that may be acute
 (sudden and reversible) or chronic (long-term and progressive). It occurs when
 digestive enzymes activate prematurely within the pancreas, leading to tissue
 autodigestion and inflammation. Acute cases can range from mild to life-
 threatening, while chronic pancreatitis causes irreversible damage, often resulting
 in diabetes and complications such as pseudocysts or infections. Causes include
 mechanical factors (e.g., gallstones, trauma), metabolic conditions (e.g., alcohol
 use, hyperlipidemia), infections, autoimmune disorders, and genetic mutations.
 Alcohol is the leading cause in men, gallstones in women, and cystic fibrosis in
 children. Risk increases with age, and disparities in care affect vulnerable groups,
 particularly Black individuals and LGBTQ+ populations (Chouairi et al., 2021).

C3. Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)
Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) is a chronic condition characterized by ulcerative
lesions in the mucosal lining of the lower esophagus, stomach, or upper
duodenum, where tissues are exposed to acid and pepsin. The main types are
gastric ulcers, typically found near the pylorus of the stomach, and duodenal
ulcers, commonly located in the proximal duodenum. Ulcers form when the
mucosal defense mechanisms break down or when there is excess acid secretion,
often leading to mucosal erosion. While gastric ulcers are less common and result
from impaired mucosal protection, duodenal ulcers are more frequent and linked
to acid hypersecretion. If left untreated, ulcers can penetrate deeper layers,
causing serious complications such as hemorrhage, perforation leading to
peritonitis, septic shock, or erosion into nearby organs like the liver, pancreas, or
biliary tract. Stress ulcers, though different, arise from physiological trauma and
are clinically distinct from chronic peptic ulcers (Wijarnpreecha et al., 2020).

C3. Gastritis
Gastritis is an inflammation of the stomach’s mucosal lining that may be localized
or widespread, and it can affect either the epithelial or glandular layers. It is
classified as acute or chronic.
In acute gastritis, inflammation is usually superficial. It
may be erosive, causing bleeding and edema, or
nonerosive, often due to Helicobacter pylori infection

multifocal atrophic gastritis (associated with gastric atrophy, ulcers, and
gastric cancer). Chronic forms can also result from bile reflux, autoimmune
                  disorders, radiation, infections, or long-term NSAID use.
                  Complications include bleeding, anemia, peptic ulcers,
                  and increased risk of gastric malignancies (Lahner et al.,

 C5. Cholecystitis and Cholelithiasis
Cholecystitis is inflammation of the gallbladder wall, which can be acute or
chronic, and is most often associated with cholelithiasis (gallstones).
Gallstones typically obstruct the cystic duct, leading to
inflammation, while biliary sludge, a thick suspension of bile-
derived crystals and salts, can also contribute to stone formation.
Though many gallstones are asymptomatic (silent), only those
causing symptoms require treatment. Gallstones are primarily
composed of cholesterol or bilirubin with calcium. Obstruction
of the gallbladder neck or duct can lead to bacterial infection
(commonly E. coli) and the release of prostaglandin-mediated
inflammatory agents.
This results in gallbladder enlargement, reduced perfusion, and, if left untreated,
may progress to ischemia, necrosis, perforation, pancreatitis, or sepsis.
Cholecystectomy (gallbladder removal) is the most common surgical procedure
for treating symptomatic gallstones (Chouairi et al., 2022).

 C6. Crohn Disease
   Crohn disease (CD) is a chronic, nonspecific inflammatory disorder that can
   affect any part of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, most commonly the
   terminal ileum, jejunum, and colon. It is marked by periods of remission
   and exacerbation and involves chronic T-cell–driven inflammation, with
   cytokines like interleukin-12 and tumor necrosis factor alpha causing tissue
   injury. CD leads to deep mucosal ulcerations, granuloma formation, and a
   characteristic cobblestone appearance of the bowel. Diseased bowel

   strictures, fistulae, GI obstruction, colon cancer, and musculoskeletal and

D1. Diabetes Mellitus
Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic disorder of carbohydrate, protein, and fat
metabolism caused by a discrepancy between insulin supply and demand. Affecting
over 120 million Americans, it is a major health burden and the seventh leading

patients are also more prone to infections and ulcers, particularly of the feet and
legs, due to impaired circulation and nerve damage. Additionally, COVID-19 has
been linked to worse outcomes in diabetic individuals due to underlying
comorbidities and inflammation (Rosenstock et al., 2020).

 Adrenal insufficiency occurs when the adrenal glands fail to produce enough

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) is a disorder of
excessive ADH release, leading to water retention and dilutional hyponatremia. It

  D8. Diabetes Insipidus
    Diabetes insipidus (DI) is a disorder marked by the excretion of large
    volumes of dilute urine (>3 L/24 hr) due to either insufficient
    antidiuretic hormone (ADH) production (central DI) or kidney resistance

    triggering thirst. If untreated, DI can cause serious complications like
    hypovolemia, hyperosmolarity, circulatory collapse, shock, and CNS
    damage, especially in patients with impaired mental alertness (Refardt et
    al., 2020).

D10. Hyperparathyroidism
Hyperparathyroidism is the overproduction of parathyroid hormone (PTH), most
commonly due to primary gland dysfunction, but it can also occur secondarily to
conditions like chronic kidney disease. PTH regulates calcium and phosphorus
balance, and its excess causes hypercalcemia and hypophosphatemia. Over time, bone
demineralization leads to osteoporosis, fractures, and soft tissue calcium deposits,
causing renal stones, renal failure, and gastrointestinal issues like peptic ulcers and
pancreatitis. Severe hyperparathyroidism also increases the risk of hypertension and
heart disease (Mallick et al., 2020).

E4. Glomerulonephritis, Acute
Acute glomerulonephritis (AGN) is a sudden inflammation of the glomeruli in the
kidneys, marked by hematuria, proteinuria, red blood cell casts, hypertension, edema,
and reduced kidney filtration. It is caused by antigen-antibody complexes, often
triggered by infections like group A streptococcal throat or skin infections. While
AGN can resolve with treatment if the infection is temporary, it can progress to
chronic kidney disease if the underlying cause persists. Major complications include
acute kidney failure, hypertensive encephalopathy, cardiac failure, and in severe cases
like rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (RPGN), rapid loss of kidney function
(Moran et al., 2021).

E5. Renal Calculi (Kidney Stones)
Renal calculi, or kidney stones, form from the crystallization of minerals in the urine
and affect about 2 million people annually in the U.S. Most stones are under 5 mm and
are composed primarily of calcium salts, though other types include struvite, uric acid,
and cystine stones. Family history increases the risk, and recurrence is common. Stones
can cause obstruction, hydronephrosis, kidney damage, and complications such as
infection, sepsis, urinary stasis, and hematuria (Haas at al., 2020).

E7. Prostate Cancer
  Prostate cancer is the most common cancer in men after skin cancer and the second

F3. Seizures / Status Epilepticus / Epilepsy
Epilepsy is a neurological syndrome characterized by recurrent seizures,
affecting about 1% of the U.S. population. It has cognitive, psychological, and
social impacts beyond the neurological effects. Seizures are caused by abnormal
electrical discharges in the brain, often involving ion movement across cell
membranes. A person can have a seizure without having epilepsy. The 2017
International League Against Epilepsy classification focuses on seizure onset
location, awareness level, and motor or nonmotor symptoms. Complications
include injury, memory loss, cardiac instability, and status epilepticus, a life-
threatening emergency involving prolonged or repeated seizures without

F4. Meningitis
Meningitis is an acute or subacute inflammation of the meninges caused by bacterial
or viral infection, often spreading from respiratory infections or open wounds.
Bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency requiring rapid antimicrobial treatment,
while viral meningitis is typically self- limiting. In bacterial meningitis, inflammation
and purulent exudate can block cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation, leading to
increased intracranial pressure, hydrocephalus, and long-term neurological damage.
Complications include cranial nerve damage, deafness, paralysis, coma, and
systemic effects like shock and hyponatremia. Pneumococcal meningitis has the
highest mortality, reaching up to 90% with severe early neurological impairment
(Ellis et al., 2020).

   F5. Encephalitis
    Encephalitis is an inflammation of the brain caused by direct infection or post-
    infectious hypersensitivity reactions, leading to swelling, hemorrhage, and nerve
    cell destruction. It primarily affects the cerebral hemispheres, brainstem, or
    cerebellum. While most forms have a mortality rate under 10%, eastern equine
    encephalitis (EEE) can reach up to 50%. Encephalitis may develop primarily
    through direct viral invasion or secondarily after viral infections or vaccinations.
    Complications can include coma, epilepsy, parkinsonism, behavioral changes,
    pneumonia, and long- term neurological impairments such as cognitive decline
    and immobility-related issues like pressure ulcers (McKnight et al., 2017).

F8. Parkinson Disease
Parkinson disease (PD) is a progressive neurological disorder affecting about
1% of adults over 70 years old. It is characterized by bradykinesia (slowed
movement), muscle rigidity, stooped posture, short shuffling steps, diminished
facial expressions, and a resting tremor. PD results from the degeneration of
dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain, disrupting
smooth motor control. Symptoms usually appear after about 80% of dopamine
is lost. Although treatments can slow progression, they do not halt it.
Complications include falls, infections, skin breakdown, and death often results
from aspiration pneumonia (Tan et al., 2020).

F9. Multiple Sclerosis

  F10. Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis
  Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive, degenerative disease that
  causes the atrophy of muscle fibers controlling voluntary movement. ALS, also
  known as Lou Gehrig's disease, is fatal, with an average life expectancy of 3
  years following diagnosis, though some may survive up to 10-15 years. The
  disease results from the progressive loss of motor neurons in the cerebral cortex
  and spinal cord, leading to muscle wasting (amyotrophy) and sclerosis (firmness
  in tissues). While sensory networks and cognitive functions remain unaffected,
  motor function deteriorates. Respiratory failure and pneumonia are common
  complications, often leading to death. Each year, 5,600 new cases are diagnosed
  in the U.S., with approximately 18,000 individuals living with the disease at any
  time (Kim et al., 2020).

F12. Myasthenia Gravis
 Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disorder that causes
 fatigue and voluntary muscle weakness, worsened by exercise and
 improved with rest. The condition occurs when antibodies block
 or reduce nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular
 junction, impairing muscle strength. Muscles commonly affected
 include those controlling eye movement, swallowing, breathing,
 and distal limb movement. MG symptoms worsen during the day
 or with stress, and it is often associated with other immune
 system or thyroid disorders. Acute exacerbations, such as
 myasthenic or cholinergic crises, can cause severe respiratory
 distress, swallowing and speaking difficulties, and generalized
 weakness. Myasthenic crisis is due to undermedication, while
 cholinergic crisis results from excessive medication. Respiratory
 distress, aspiration pneumonia, and poor nutrition are major
 complications of MG (Alanazy et al., 2020).

F1A. Iron deficiency Anemia
Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) is the most common type of anemia, caused by
insufficient iron intake, poor storage, or excessive loss. This leads to pale, small
red blood cells that cannot effectively carry oxygen. IDA is defined by
hemoglobin levels below 12 g/dL in women and 14 g/dL in men. It is more
prevalent in economically disadvantaged individuals due to limited access to
iron-rich foods. Complications include infections, pneumonia, hypoxemia, and
exacerbation of coronary artery disease. In severe cases, conditions like
Plummer-Vinson syndrome, characterized by difficulty swallowing and
spooning of nails, may develop, especially in middle-aged women with poor
nutrition (Li et al., 2020).

   F2A. Sickle Cell Disease/Anemia
   Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a genetic disorder that affects the hemoglobin in
   red blood cells, causing them to become rigid and sickle-shaped under low
   oxygen conditions. This leads to a shortened lifespan of the cells, reduced
   oxygen delivery, and blockage of small blood vessels, causing pain and
   potential organ damage. SCD is most common among Black Americans, with
   approximately 100,000 people affected in the U.S. Despite its challenges,
   individuals with SCD have some protection against malaria. The disease can
   lead to serious complications like stroke, heart failure, and chronic pulmonary
   disease, and life expectancy is often limited (Cisnero & Thein, 2020).

G3A. Hemophilia

 Hemophilia is a group of inherited bleeding disorders caused by deficiencies in
 clotting factors, most commonly factor VIII (Hemophilia A) or factor IX
 (Hemophilia B). Hemophilia A affects about 1 in 5,000 to 10,000 male births,
 while Hemophilia B is rarer. Hemophilia C, due to factor XI deficiency, is the
 rarest form. Individuals with hemophilia can form platelet plugs but cannot form
 stable clots, leading to prolonged bleeding. Severity depends on clotting factor
 levels, with severe cases requiring frequent therapy. Recurrent bleeding, especially
 into joints (hemarthrosis), can cause pain, deformities, and disability, and even
 minor trauma may result in life-threatening hemorrhage (Shima et al., 2019).

   Inheritance: Passed from carrier mothers to sons
   New Mutations: ~1/3 of cases have no family history—caused by spontaneous

G3B. Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation
 Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is a life-threatening condition
 where abnormal clotting and bleeding occur simultaneously. Triggered by
 various underlying conditions, DIC involves widespread activation of the
 clotting cascade, leading to the formation of microclots that block capillaries and

 especially in severely ill or injured patients. Complications include hemorrhage,
 organ failure, shock, and death (Ikezoe, 2021).

 G4. Blood Transfusion Reaction
 Blood transfusion reactions are adverse responses to any blood component and
 may occur acutely (within 24 hours) or be delayed. They range from mild
 allergic reactions like hives and fever to severe, life-threatening complications
 such as acute hemolytic reactions, anaphylaxis, circulatory overload, or
 transfusion-related lung injury. Most fatal hemolytic reactions result from
 human error. Reactions can be immune-mediated or due to nonimmune factors
 like improper storage. Symptoms vary by type and may include fever, chills,
 dyspnea, hypotension, or shock. Despite blood matching, incompatibilities can
 still occur. Severe complications include renal failure, DIC, and increased
 infection risk due to immune suppression (Shmookler & Flanagan, 2020).




Causes
    Immune Response: Recipient's immune system attacks donor proteins,
    especially with repeated transfusions.

    Nonimmune Causes: Improper storage, handling, or administration of
    blood components.

    Hemolytic Reaction: Occurs with ABO incompatibility, often due to
    misidentification or name confusion.

High-Risk Patients:
  Massive transfusions (e.g., trauma, liver transplants).
  Frequent transfusions (e.g., leukemia, sickle cell disease) → increased
  antibody formation and sensitivity over time.

G6. Idiopathic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP)
Idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) is an acquired bleeding disorder
caused by the immune system producing antibodies that destroy platelets,
leading to a low platelet count. It occurs in two forms: acute ITP, mostly in

kidneys or abdominal organs. Prognosis is excellent for children and generally
good for adults, though older age and a history of bleeding increase the risk of
severe complications (Grace et al., 2019).

 F8A. Anaphylaxis

     Anaphylactic shock, or anaphylaxis, is a rapid, life-threatening allergic
     reaction involving multiple organ systems, triggered by a systemic immune
     response to an allergen. It typically occurs after prior sensitization to an
     antigen, with reexposure causing a severe IgE-mediated reaction that leads
     to the release of histamine and other mediators, resulting in bronchospasm,
     vasodilation, and cardiovascular collapse. New definitions now include non-
     IgE and non- immunologic forms. Most reactions happen within minutes,
     with airway obstruction and hypotension being the leading causes of death.
     Up to 15% of North Americans are at risk, and 1,000 deaths occur annually
     in the U.S (Turner et al., 2020).

G9A. Lupus Erythematosus
 Lupus erythematosus is a chronic autoimmune disease that affects connective tissue
and can involve the kidneys, blood, and nervous system. It has two main forms:
systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), which affects multiple organs, and discoid
lupus erythematosus (DLE), which is limited to the skin. SLE causes
inflammation due to autoantibodies that form immune complexes, damaging organs
like the skin and kidneys. The disease follows a pattern of flares and remissions and
can be fatal, with renal failure and infection being the leading causes of death.
Thanks to improved treatments, many patients now live 25–30 years after diagnosis
(Castellano-Rioja et al., 2020).

                                     Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic,

 ABOUT THE AUTHOR

Hi! I'm Alex K. Korir—an experienced critical care nurse and an
educator with over a decade of hands-on nursing experience. As a child,
I always dreamed of being a nurse, but then I realized my dreams could
come true: To be a nurse and an educator. I graduated from The
University of Alabama, Tuscaloosa (2008), Bishop State, Mobile,
Alabama (2013), and Western Governors University, Utah (2024). I've
worked in some of the most prestigious Magnet-designated hospitals,
including Keck Medicine of USC in Los Angeles and the University of
California Medical Center in Orange, California. My background spans
med-surg, oncology, telemetry, and intensive care units, and I hold a
Master of Science in Nursing. I also have certifications in critical care
and cardiac medicine (CCRN-CMC) with the American Critical Care
Association.

I wrote this book to help simplify complex nursing concepts and give
students and professionals the confidence they need, whether they are
preparing for the NCLEX or advancing in clinical practice. My goal is
to make learning clear, practical, and empowering.

Outside of nursing, I enjoy traveling, trading stocks, reading medical
journals, and volunteering to support unhoused individuals in my
community. Thanks for checking out my book. I hope it becomes a
helpful part of your nursing journey!
